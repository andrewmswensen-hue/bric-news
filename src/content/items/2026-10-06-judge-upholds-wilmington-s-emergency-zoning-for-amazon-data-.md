title: "Judge upholds Wilmington's emergency zoning for Amazon data center"
summary: "A Clinton County judge dismissed a homeowner's lawsuit challenging two emergency zoning ordinances that cleared the way for Amazon Web Services' proposed $4 billion data center in Wilmington, Ohio, WLWT reported. The ordinances, which rezoned more than 470 acres of farmland to light industrial use and permit data centers there, carried emergency clauses that took effect immediately and bypassed a public referendum. Judge Jonathan Hein ruled the city provided sufficient reasons, citing effects on infrastructure, utilities, planning and zoning. The homeowner's attorneys plan to appeal."
detail: "Wilmington City Council approved zoning ordinances in 2024 and 2025 to clear a path for Amazon's data center proposal, then repealed and replaced them over the summer after homeowners alleged in federal court that the original approvals violated Ohio's open meetings law, according to WLWT. The replacement ordinances kept the same substance, permitting data centers as a use in light industrial zones and rezoning more than 470 acres of Amazon-owned farmland, but added emergency clauses that take effect immediately and are not subject to a voter referendum.\n\nWilmington homeowner Rachel Brooks challenged the emergency clauses in Clinton County Court, arguing the city used the emergency language specifically to avoid a ballot referendum. Ohio law allows cities to pass emergency measures necessary for the immediate preservation of public peace, health or safety, and the Ohio Supreme Court has held that municipalities must explain their reasons for declaring an emergency. Judge Jonathan Hein ruled the city's cited reasons, covering infrastructure, utilities, planning and zoning, were sufficient, and dismissed the case. Brooks' attorneys disagree with that reading and plan to appeal."
why_it_matters: "The ruling is a reminder that an Ohio city council can use an emergency clause to put a rezoning into effect immediately and keep it off the ballot, a tool any Columbus-area suburb could reach for the next time a large commercial or data center project needs fast-tracked zoning."
