title: "Athens County reappraisals show how Ohio's tax relief laws work"
summary: "Athens County's 2026 reappraisal came in about 19% below sale prices on average, county auditor Jill Davidson said, even as most home values rose sharply from the last reappraisal three years ago. New taxes from the higher values take effect next year. Athens broker Ally Rapp Lee said rising taxes are pricing some buyers out of homes they could otherwise afford."
detail: "Athens County Auditor Jill Davidson said her office intentionally appraises homes below full market value because the reappraisal process, conducted every three years, relies on sales data that is already two to three years old by the time new values are mailed to owners. A WOUB analysis of 170 open-market single-family home sales from January through August found the median sale price was $271,250, about 19% above the new 2026 appraised values on average, and nearly a third of homes sold for more than $100,000 above their 2023 appraisal.\n\nProperty taxes in Ohio are calculated on 35% of a home's appraised value, multiplied by the local tax rate in mills, then reduced by credits. State law from the 1970s automatically lowers the millage on many voter-approved levies as property values rise, so a levy generally continues to raise about the same dollar amount it did when voters approved it rather than growing with appraisals. School operating levies are the exception: their combined millage cannot fall below a 20-mill floor, so in districts already near that floor, rising values translate more directly into higher tax bills."
why_it_matters: "Franklin County landlords going through their own reappraisal cycle can use Athens County's math as a preview: unless a taxing district is already at Ohio's 20-mill floor for school levies, most of a rising appraisal is offset by falling millage on existing levies, so the real tax increase is usually smaller than the appraisal jump alone suggests."
