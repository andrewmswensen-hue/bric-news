title: "Ohio rentals: which fire code sections govern smoke and CO alarms"
summary: "Ohio rentals need working smoke alarms under the Ohio Fire Code, and carbon monoxide alarms when a unit meets any of four conditions in fire code section 915. Existing buildings had a January 1, 2019 CO deadline. ORC 5321.04 turns those code rules into a landlord duty. Columbus adds its own requirements, including hard-wired detectors in one- to three-family buildings built after April 26, 1978."
detail: "RL Property Management's explainer says the authority for rental smoke alarms is the Ohio Fire Code (OAC 1301:7-7-11), not ORC 3781.104, which the publisher says applies to buildings over 75 feet tall. Existing rental buildings, classed as Group R, must have single-station smoke alarms, and battery-only alarms are permitted in existing buildings where no construction is taking place.\n\nCarbon monoxide alarms are required when a unit meets any one of four conditions: it contains a fuel-burning appliance or fireplace, it is served by a fuel-burning forced-air furnace, it sits in a building with fuel-burning appliances elsewhere and communicating openings, or the building has an attached private garage. Existing buildings had until January 1, 2019 to install them, and battery-only CO alarms are allowed there.\n\nORC 5321.04(A)(1) and (A)(2) make code compliance and habitability the owner's duty, and the publisher notes a violation can amount to negligence per se. ORC 5321.05 gives residents a matching duty to keep their space safe and sanitary.\n\nColumbus is stricter and more specific. Under Columbus City Code 4529.08 the owner installs required detectors, and hard-wired units are required in one- to three-family buildings built after April 26, 1978. Under 4529.11, in a one- to three-family dwelling the occupant is responsible for operation and maintenance. The 10-year replacement guidance comes from NFPA 72, manufacturers, and the U.S. Fire Administration rather than Ohio law. The publisher states the article is informational and not legal advice. The publisher also flags that one CO trigger catches owners off guard: a unit with an all-electric kitchen can still need CO detection if a gas water heater sits in a shared basement with a communicating opening."
why_it_matters: "Citing the wrong section matters when an inspector or attorney asks what was done, and the Columbus hard-wiring rule for one- to three-family buildings built after April 1978 goes beyond the state's allowance for battery-only alarms."
