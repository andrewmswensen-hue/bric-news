title: "Piqua and Trenton Data Center Bans Test Ohio Zoning Precedent"
summary: "Voters in Piqua and Trenton, Ohio approved charter amendments banning large data centers Nov. 3, raising questions about projects already underway. Piqua is reviewing how its ban affects an approved Meta project; Trenton residents sent the city a formal demand to revoke a zoning certificate for an Amazon Web Services campus already under construction. Both cases turn on an October Ohio Supreme Court ruling involving Lordstown, which held a developer can gain vested rights to a permit once it meets the legal requirements in place at the time it applied."
detail: "Two Ohio cities passed charter amendments restricting large data centers on Nov. 3, part of a wave of ten such Ohio votes tracked by the group Conserve Ohio, according to the Dayton Daily News. Piqua's amendment, approved 66.7% to 33.3%, bars data centers using more than 25 megawatts and leaves the city reviewing what it means for a Meta project already approved in the I-75 Business and Industrial Park, which the city estimates at more than $1 billion in investment. Trenton's measure passed with nearly 80% support; five residents sent the city a Nov. 9 demand letter asking it to seek a court order revoking the zoning certificate issued in December 2025 for a four-building Amazon Web Services campus, arguing the charter's definition of construction covers ongoing site work. The developer, Prologis, said the vote does not affect the project. Both disputes turn on an October Ohio Supreme Court ruling in a Lordstown case, which found a developer can acquire vested rights to a permit once it has met the legal requirements in place at the time of application, even during a later moratorium. David Montgomery, a land-use attorney with Coolidge Wall Co., told the paper that a project with an issued permit or construction underway has a stronger legal argument to continue than one still awaiting approval. A separate Amazon Web Services project proposed on roughly 600 acres in nearby townships, and zoning moves in Butler County to restrict data centers as a primary land use, show the fight extending beyond the two cities. The article also notes an existing AWS data center in Dublin, a Columbus suburb."
why_it_matters: "Columbus already has its own data center footprint, including an AWS facility in Dublin, so the vested-rights standard the Ohio Supreme Court is applying in these disputes, whether a project with permits or construction underway can proceed despite a later zoning ban, will likely govern any similar fight a Central Ohio municipality picks with a developer."
