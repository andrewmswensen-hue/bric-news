title: "Why a Short-Term Rental's Headline Yield Can Mislead: The Gross-to-Cash Math"
summary: "BiggerPockets ranked the best short-term rental market in every state by modeled gross yield (annual revenue divided by home value), drawing on BNBCalc's analysis of 462 cities and 10 million Airbnb and VRBO listings, and found the median top-ranked market yields 10.91%. But running that median property's numbers through financing, operating costs and a property manager's cut turns a 10.91% gross yield into roughly a 2.5% cash-on-cash return before management, and a loss once a manager is added."
detail: "The analysis, built on BNBCalc data covering 462 cities across all 50 states with at least 50 properties each, ranks the best short-term rental city in every state by modeled gross yield: annual short-term rental revenue divided by the city's median home value for a given bedroom count, with unusual outlier properties filtered out. The author argues gross yield is useful precisely because it skips the assumptions, interest rate, down payment, expense ratio, management or self-management, that make cash-on-cash comparisons unreliable across different investors and deals.\n\nThe piece then works a real example: the median top-ranked property costs $251,338 and produces $27,671 in annual revenue, a 10.91% gross yield. At 20% down ($50,267), financed at 7% over 30 years, debt service runs $16,052 a year. Operating expenses (cleaning, supplies, utilities, insurance, taxes and repairs) at 37.5% of revenue take another $10,376, leaving about $1,241, a 2.5% cash-on-cash return. Hiring a property manager at a typical 20% of gross revenue, $5,534 on this property, turns that thin margin into an annual loss of roughly $4,292. The takeaway is that a market's advertised yield only tells an investor where to start looking, not what a specific deal will actually pay out once real financing and operating costs are applied."
why_it_matters: "The same gross-to-cash math applies to any Columbus-area short-term rental an investor is evaluating: run the advertised yield through financing, a realistic 35 to 40% expense ratio and a manager's cut before assuming a headline number reflects real cash flow."
