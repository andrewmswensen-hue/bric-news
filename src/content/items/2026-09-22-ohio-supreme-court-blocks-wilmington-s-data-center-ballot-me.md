title: "Ohio Supreme Court Blocks Wilmington's Data Center Ballot Measure"
summary: "The Ohio Supreme Court ruled 6-1 that a citizen-led ballot measure to regulate data centers in Wilmington cannot appear on the November ballot, siding with the Clinton County Board of Elections. The proposed ordinance would have let nearby residents sue data center operators directly over noise, water use, chemical discharge, light pollution and decommissioning, with courts able to issue fines, injunctions and shutdowns. The majority held that a municipal ballot initiative cannot create new legal causes of action, a power reserved to the state."
detail: "The ruling closes out a monthslong petition drive in Wilmington, a Clinton County city that has become a center of Ohio's data center boom. Organizers with the citizen group behind the effort gathered signatures over the summer for an ordinance that would have imposed local rules on data center noise, water use, chemical discharge, light pollution, generator testing, heat generation and long-term decommissioning. The proposal's enforcement mechanism, allowing residents to sue operators directly and letting courts impose daily fines, injunctions and shutdowns, was the part the Ohio Supreme Court's majority found unlawful: the court held that a municipal citizen initiative cannot create a new legal cause of action, since that authority belongs to the state legislature. Justice Michael Fischer dissented, arguing that a county board of elections should not be deciding complex legal questions about a proposed law before voters get to weigh in on it, and that judicial review should come after enactment instead. Organizer Quintin Koger Kidd, who helped lead the petition drive, called the outcome disappointing and said the proposal offered protections the current city council had not provided. He pointed to Fischer's dissent as evidence the legal question of how much authority boards of elections have to block ballot measures remains unsettled in Ohio."
why_it_matters: "The ruling does not resolve whether Ohio cities can regulate data centers, only that citizens cannot write private lawsuit rights into a local ballot ordinance, so a Columbus-area investor watching data center fights in Grove City, Granville or Pataskala should expect future local rules to come through city council votes or state legislation rather than citizen-initiated ordinances with built-in enforcement teeth."
