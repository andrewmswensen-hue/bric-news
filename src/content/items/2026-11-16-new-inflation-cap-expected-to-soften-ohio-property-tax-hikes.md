title: "New Inflation Cap Expected to Soften Ohio Property Tax Hikes This Cycle"
summary: "Triennial and sexennial property reappraisals in Hamilton, Butler, and Clermont counties show values still rising, 27 percent for Hamilton County residential properties, 19 percent in Butler County, and 23 percent overall in Clermont County, though smaller than the roughly 34 percent jump Hamilton County saw in 2023. County auditors say a 2025 state law, House Bill 186, caps how much a property's taxable value can grow annually tied to local inflation, which should limit the sharp tax-bill increases seen three years ago."
detail: "Hamilton County is completing its three-year update to valuations for more than 350,000 parcels this month, while Butler and Clermont counties finished six-year full reevaluations this year. Hamilton County's overall property values rose 21 percent, with residential properties up 27 percent, down from a 34 percent increase in 2023. Butler County's overall values rose 20 percent, with residential properties up 19 percent, compared with 37 percent three years ago. Clermont County's values rose 23 percent overall.\n\nAuditors in both counties said a bigger valuation increase will not translate directly into a proportional tax increase this cycle because of House Bill 186, a 2025 state law that caps how much a property's taxable value can grow each year based on local inflation. Butler County Auditor Nancy Nix said she does not expect any property to see more than a 10 percent tax increase this time, unlike the sticker shock of 2023. Hamilton County property owners could still see increases from other sources, including a new child-services levy and a Cincinnati Public Schools operating levy passed this month.\n\nThe reappraisals come as opponents of Ohio property taxes prepare another push for a statewide ballot measure to abolish them, after a similar effort narrowly failed earlier this year, according to WKRC."
why_it_matters: "House Bill 186's inflation cap is now the backstop that keeps a big valuation jump from turning into an equally big tax bill, so an owner anywhere in Ohio facing a reappraisal this year should check the cap before assuming their bill will track the new value."
