title: "Athens County's 2026 Reappraisal Shows Gap Between Home Values and Tax Bills"
summary: "Athens County's 2026 property reappraisal set new home values that, on average, still run about 19 percent below actual sale prices for the 170 single-family homes WOUB analyzed through August, meaning most owners will keep paying less than full market value in property tax for now. Auditor Jill Davidson said the appraisals lag the market because the office relies on sales from two to three years earlier and does not inspect interiors. Ohio's 20-mill floor and recent state relief laws will shape how much of the gap eventually reaches tax bills."
detail: "Athens County's median home sale price from January through August 2026 was $271,250, about 19 percent above the new 2026 auditor appraisal on average, and nearly a third of homes sold for more than $100,000 over their prior 2023 appraised value. Auditor Jill Davidson said her office intentionally appraises with a margin of caution because reappraisals, done every three years, rely on sales data that is already two to three years old and exclude interior inspections, so undisclosed renovations are not captured. She warned homeowners who challenge a reappraisal as too high risk having it raised instead, particularly if they paid more than the appraised value. Property taxes are calculated on 35 percent of appraised value, multiplied by local millage; Ohio law reduces the effective millage of most levies as values rise so taxpayers pay roughly the same amount approved by voters, except for school levies, which are protected by a 20-mill floor that keeps them from falling as far. The piece explains that new state relief laws will shift how much of the value increase converts to higher bills, with the next reappraisal in three years likely to bring another jump if prices keep rising."
why_it_matters: "The same reappraisal mechanics and 20-mill floor apply in Franklin, Delaware and Licking counties, so an investor who successfully disputes a low appraisal risks a correction upward rather than down, and should expect further tax increases at the next reappraisal if local home values keep climbing."
