detail: "House Bill 999, introduced this month by Rep. Daniel Troy (D-Willowick), would bar any state or local agency in Ohio from granting property tax exemptions to data centers, and would direct a review of whether existing abatements can be ended. Troy has been working with Rep. David Thomas (R-Jefferson) on reducing Ohioans' property tax burden; Thomas has spoken favorably about changing data center abatements but is not a sponsor of this bill. Abatements are typically granted at the local level and vary widely. Columbus approved 15 years of exemption for a Google data center in 2021, an estimated $54 million break. Hilliard gave Amazon $5.4 million in exemptions in 2024. New Albany, a hub for data center development, typically grants 10 to 15 year abatements, with larger projects reaching 30 years, and requires a minimum annual payment regardless of the exemption. A Scioto Analysis survey of 40 Ohio university economists published Sept. 21 found 62 percent think local abatements are effective at attracting development, while 27 percent disagreed. The bill goes further than House Bill 646, an earlier measure that would have capped abatements at 50 percent and required enforcement of development promises; that bill nearly passed before stalling over separate disagreements on the state's sales tax exemption for data centers, which Troy said has cost the state $1.6 billion since 2025. HB 999 awaits a committee assignment and is unlikely to see action before the November election."
why_it_matters: "If data centers stop receiving property tax exemptions, the full value of those properties returns to the local tax base, which could ease the pressure on tax rates that school districts and counties otherwise pass on to other property owners, including Columbus-area landlords."
