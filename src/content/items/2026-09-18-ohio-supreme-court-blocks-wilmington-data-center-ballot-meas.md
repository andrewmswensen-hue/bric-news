title: "Ohio Supreme Court Blocks Wilmington Data Center Ballot Measure"
summary: "The Ohio Supreme Court ruled that a citizen-led zoning initiative to regulate data centers in Wilmington cannot appear on the November ballot because it improperly created a private right for residents to sue data center operators over zoning violations. The court found that creating such a right of action exceeds what a municipality can enact through a ballot initiative, citing a 2018 precedent involving a Columbus fracking ban. Justice Patrick Fischer dissented, arguing courts, not boards of elections, should decide whether a ballot measure is legal before an election."
detail: "The Supreme Court of Ohio ruled that a citizen initiative to add data center zoning regulations to Wilmington's city code cannot go before voters on the November 3 ballot. Residents Quintin Koger Kidd and Susan Croutwater had proposed a new zoning chapter setting standards for noise, lighting, emissions, water use and generator testing at data centers, enforceable through a private right of action that would let residents sue operators directly and recover fines up to $10,000 a day.\n\nThe Clinton County Board of Elections refused to certify the measure after the city argued the private-lawsuit provision exceeded what a municipality can enact by ballot initiative. In a per curiam opinion, a Supreme Court majority agreed, citing a 2018 case in which the court blocked a Columbus ballot measure banning fracking-related activity for the same reason: cities cannot use ballot initiatives to create new legal rights to sue.\n\nJustice Jennifer Brunner concurred separately. Justice Patrick Fischer dissented, writing that election boards are making legal determinations they are not constitutionally empowered to make, and that voters should be allowed to approve a measure before a court reviews its legality.\n\nThe ruling sets precedent limiting how far any Ohio municipality, including those in Central Ohio weighing similar data center rules, can go in giving residents direct legal power to enforce local zoning."
why_it_matters: "Any Central Ohio city or township drafting its own data center or large-development zoning rules now knows a citizen-enforcement provision, letting residents sue violators directly, will not survive a ballot vote, narrowing the tools communities have to regulate development beyond city-led enforcement."
