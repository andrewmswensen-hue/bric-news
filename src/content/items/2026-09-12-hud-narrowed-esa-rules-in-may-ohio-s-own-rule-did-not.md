title: "HUD Narrowed ESA Rules in May; Ohio's Own Rule Did Not"
summary: "A May 22, 2026 HUD enforcement memo narrowed federal fair housing enforcement to assistance animals individually trained to perform a disability-related task, ending HUD's practice of treating untrained emotional support animals the same as service animals. Ohio's own civil rights rule was not changed: Ohio Administrative Code 4112-5-07 still protects any \"animal assistant\" that aids a person with a disability and still bars landlords from charging an extra fee for one."
detail: "On May 22, 2026, HUD Assistant Secretary Craig Trainor issued an enforcement memo narrowing the agency's fair housing enforcement to assistance animals \"individually trained to perform work or tasks directly related to the complainant's disability,\" explicitly excluding animals that provide only emotional support, comfort or companionship. The memo followed HUD's September 2025 withdrawal of its 2020 assistance-animal guidance. It changes how HUD investigates complaints, not the underlying Fair Housing Act, so residents can still sue directly in court under the statute, and Section 504 and state law are unaffected. Ohio's own rule did not move. Ohio Administrative Code 4112-5-07(C) still entitles a person with a disability to keep an \"animal assistant\" on the property with no extra charge, and the adjoining definition at OAC 4112-5-02(C) defines an animal assistant as \"any animal which aids the persons with a disability,\" language that does not require training. The Ohio Civil Rights Commission, which investigates most Columbus-area fair housing complaints as a HUD-funded state agency, had published no position on how the federal shift affects its own rule as of September 2026. That leaves an open question for owners: whether Ohio's broader definition still requires waiving pet fees for an untrained emotional support animal, even though HUD itself no longer requires it. Legal commentary since May has advised owners against denying requests categorically or revoking previously approved accommodations without new facts about the animal, since a blanket denial remains the highest-risk response under both the old and new frameworks. Direct threat and property damage remain valid grounds for denial under both federal and Ohio law, but each requires evidence tied to the specific animal rather than assumptions based on breed or size."
why_it_matters: "A Columbus owner who reads only the HUD memo and starts charging pet fees for emotional support animals could still be violating Ohio's broader civil rights rule, which the Ohio Civil Rights Commission has not yet said it will read the same way HUD now does."
