title: "Columbus weighs a new water surcharge for data centers and big industrial users"
summary: "Columbus Water and Power is considering a \"peaking rate\" surcharge on its highest-volume industrial water customers, including data centers, chip fabrication plants, and brewing and food processing facilities, to recover the cost of building and maintaining the larger pipe capacity those users require. The city says the proposal, in development for two years, would not affect residential customers. Council has legislation on first reading directing Water and Power to bring a formal peak-rate proposal to council before the end of October."
detail: "Columbus Water and Power Director Kristen Atha said some industrial customers need large-diameter water lines, in one example a 12-inch line, built and kept ready to deliver a high volume of water even though they do not draw that full volume every day. The proposed peaking rate would charge those customers for that standby capacity rather than spreading the cost across all ratepayers. Deputy Director Jon Lee said the utility, as a municipal system, sets rates to cover its cost of service rather than to generate profit, and is now evaluating what a peak charge on high-volume users would need to recover. The city published a study on the proposal's impact in June, and Water and Power will need to work with the city's Utility Advisory Board before bringing specific charges to council. Councilmember Christopher Wyche said the ordinance now on first reading directs Water and Power to develop a new rate recommendation for data centers and other high-volume users and grew out of a series of public hearings on data centers, the third of which is scheduled this week. Wyche said residents have been clear that they do not want to keep subsidizing water infrastructure built to serve large industrial expansion."
why_it_matters: "The proposal explicitly excludes residential landlords, but it shows Columbus moving toward charging the users who strain its water system for the infrastructure they require, a cost-recovery model that could reach mixed-use or light industrial rental property if a future rate case broadens who counts as high-volume."
