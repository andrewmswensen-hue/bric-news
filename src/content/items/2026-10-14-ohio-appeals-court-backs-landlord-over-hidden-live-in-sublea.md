title: "Ohio appeals court backs landlord over hidden live-in sublease in warehouse"
summary: "The Ohio Ninth District Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for a landlord in Stop 65, LLC v. Beach Park Railway, LLC, a dispute over a 15,000-square-foot leased suite in an Avon Lake shopping center. Beach Park's lease barred subletting without written consent, but a third party moved in, ran a private motorcycle repair business, and lived there with a bed, appliances and a pet, all undisputed at trial. The October 5, 2026 ruling held this arrangement was a sublease, not a mere license, rejecting Beach Park's waiver and notice defenses and upholding the eviction."
detail: "Beach Park Railway, LLC held a lease on a 15,000-square-foot suite in an Avon Lake shopping center to operate a museum, under an agreement with the Ohio Facilities Commission. The lease barred subletting any part of the premises without the landlord's written approval. According to Mortgage Professional America, a third party whose own facility had been destroyed by a storm began helping at the museum around September 2023 and the arrangement grew from there. By spring 2024, the landlord's representative found the person running a private motorcycle repair business inside the suite, complete with parts inventory and a repair ramp, plus a bed, washer, dryer, oven, refrigerator and a pet, and receiving personal mail at the address. Beach Park argued this was a license, not a sublease, and that paying $1,100 toward utilities did not change that. The Ohio Ninth District Court of Appeals disagreed, finding the occupant's use covered 'all purposes a tenant would enjoy,' which makes it a sublease regardless of what the parties called it. The court also rejected Beach Park's waiver and notice defenses, including a claim that the landlord owed an additional 30-day self-help notice before pursuing a statutory eviction. All six of Beach Park's assigned errors were rejected, and the eviction was affirmed."
why_it_matters: "For Ohio landlords, this ruling confirms that an occupant's actual use of a space, not what anyone calls the arrangement, determines whether it is an enforceable sublease, which matters anytime a tenant lets an unauthorized third party move in or run a business out of leased space."
