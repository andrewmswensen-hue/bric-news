title: "Ohio reappraisals push some county property values up 22% this year"
summary: "More than 40 Ohio counties updated property values this year, with some seeing sharp increases after routine reappraisals, The Statehouse News Bureau reported. In Clermont County, countywide residential values rose nearly $4 billion, an average 22% increase, about double the 11.8% rise recorded at the 2020 reappraisal. County Auditor Linda Fraley said rising home sale prices drove the increase, with the median residential sale price up nearly $70,000 since the 2023 update."
detail: "Ohio counties conduct full property reappraisals on a six-year cycle with an update at the three-year midpoint, and this year's cycle brought steep increases in some areas. The Statehouse News Bureau reported that Clermont County's residential values rose by nearly $4 billion countywide, an average 22% increase, about double the 11.8% rise recorded at the county's last full reappraisal in 2020. County Auditor Linda Fraley attributed the jump mainly to sale prices, saying the median residential sale price climbed nearly $70,000 since the 2023 midpoint update.\n\nTax bills are calculated on assessed value, which is 35% of the appraised market value homeowners saw in this summer's reappraisal notices, not the larger figure itself. Fraley said her office cannot yet estimate 2026 tax bills because of new state laws that change the calculation: one limits how much schools may collect based on inflation rather than rising property values and expands the existing owner-occupancy credit, and another caps how much inside millage levies not approved by voters can grow. Fraley expects estimates by December, with bills due in February. Property owners who believe their valuation is too high can file an appeal with their county's Board of Revision between Jan. 1 and March 31."
why_it_matters: "Property tax bills are based on assessed value, just 35% of a home's appraised market value, so a large reappraisal jump does not translate dollar for dollar into a tax increase, and the new state cap on inflation-based school collections adds another buffer landlords in a reappraisal year should factor into next year's budget rather than assuming a straight-line increase."
