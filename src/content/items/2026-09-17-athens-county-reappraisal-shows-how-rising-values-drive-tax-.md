title: "Athens County Reappraisal Shows How Rising Values Drive Tax Bills"
summary: "Athens County's 2026 property reappraisal shows home values still running below actual market prices even after a significant increase, meaning most homeowners will pay less than they technically owe this cycle but face another steep jump at the next reappraisal in three years if prices keep rising. Auditor Jill Davidson said her office bases values on sales from two to three years earlier, so appraisals structurally lag a fast-moving market. The county's median home sale price was $271,250 from January through August, well above what the average local wage earner can affordably finance."
detail: "Athens County's 2026 reappraisal letters show most home values rising since the last reappraisal three years ago, in many cases by tens of thousands of dollars, according to Auditor Jill Davidson. Even so, the new values remain below actual market prices in many cases, because the state process relies on home sales from two to three years earlier and the auditor's office does not inspect interiors. WOUB's analysis of 170 single-family home sales from January through August found a median sale price of $271,250, about 19 percent above the new appraised values on average, with nearly a third of homes selling for more than $100,000 over their prior appraisal.\n\nProperty taxes in Ohio are calculated on 35 percent of a home's appraised value, multiplied by a tax rate set by local levies. Because many levies are millage-based and tied to property values, a rising appraisal increases the dollar amount collected under a levy even without a new vote, until a district's levies hit the state's 20-mill floor, after which revenue grows directly with property values instead of being offset. Davidson said the same mechanics will apply statewide at the next reappraisal in three years unless home prices level off, though state lawmakers have been working on relief measures in response to public frustration over rising bills."
why_it_matters: "Landlords anywhere in Ohio holding property in a district already at the 20-mill floor should expect their tax bill to track home-value growth going forward instead of being cushioned by declining millage, the same mechanism now showing up in Athens County's reappraisal."
