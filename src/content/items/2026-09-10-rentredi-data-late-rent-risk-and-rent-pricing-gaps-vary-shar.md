title: "RentRedi data: late-rent risk and rent-pricing gaps vary sharply by state"
summary: "A BiggerPockets interview with RentRedi co-founder Ed Barone, drawing on data from close to 200,000 landlords and renters on the platform, finds late-payment rates swing as much as 4x by state, from around 5% in Utah and Hawaii to 20% in Mississippi. The piece also estimates the cost of holding rent flat for a good tenant: on a $1,500 unit, RentRedi's data puts average rent growth at about 46% over roughly 6.8 years, meaning five years of frozen rent can mean thousands in forgone income per unit."
detail: "Barone's read on the late-payment data is that a landlord with only a few units should not treat every late payment as a verdict on the tenant, since some of the gap tracks to where the property sits rather than tenant quality. He recommends a simple, consistently applied system rather than case-by-case judgment calls: set autopay as the default at lease signing, send reminders before the due date rather than after, and write a specific, fixed late fee into the lease that gets charged every time rent is late, not only sometimes. The article's Texas example shows how much state law constrains that fee: under Texas Property Code 92.019, a landlord cannot collect a late fee at all unless it is written into the lease and rent is at least two full days late, and the fee is only presumed reasonable up to 12% of monthly rent in a building of four units or fewer, or 10% in larger buildings; charging beyond that can expose a landlord to a tenant claim for $100, three times the amount wrongly collected, plus attorney's fees. On the pricing side, RentRedi's data shows average unit rents climbing about 46% over roughly 6.8 years; the article estimates that holding a $1,500 unit flat for five years costs a landlord in the neighborhood of $6,000 in forgone rent, and argues the true number often runs higher once compounding is factored in."
why_it_matters: "Ohio sets its own limits on what a landlord can charge as a late fee, different from the Texas cap used as the example here, so Columbus-area landlords without a written, consistently enforced late-fee clause should confirm their own lease matches Ohio's statute rather than assume this national data applies as written."
