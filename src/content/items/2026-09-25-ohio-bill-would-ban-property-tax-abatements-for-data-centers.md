title: "Ohio Bill Would Ban Property Tax Abatements for Data Centers"
summary: "State Rep. Daniel Troy introduced House Bill 999, which would bar state and local agencies from granting new property tax exemptions to data centers and would examine whether existing abatements can be terminated. Troy said ending the breaks would shift some tax burden off residential property owners. A Scioto Analysis survey of 40 Ohio university economists published Sept. 21 found 62 percent think local abatements are effective at attracting data center development, while 27 percent disagreed."
detail: "House Bill 999 would prohibit any state or local agency from granting new property tax exemptions to data centers and directs a review of whether existing abatement agreements can be ended early. Troy, a Democrat from Willowick, has been working with Republican Rep. David Thomas of Jefferson on the property tax question, though Thomas is not a cosponsor of this bill. Property tax abatements for data centers are granted locally and vary widely: Columbus approved a 15-year exemption worth an estimated $54 million for a Google data center in 2021, Hilliard's Amazon facilities saved $5.4 million in property taxes in 2024, and New Albany, a major data center hub, typically grants 10-to-15-year abatements with larger projects reaching 30 years, along with a minimum annual payment requirement. The bill goes further than an earlier proposal, House Bill 646, which would have capped abatements at 50 percent and required enforcement of investment promises rather than banning them outright. HB 646 nearly passed before lawmakers left for summer break but stalled over a separate dispute involving the state's sales tax exemption for data centers, which Gov. Mike DeWine paused indefinitely for new applicants in June, though existing agreements remain in place."
why_it_matters: "If House Bill 999 or a version of it passes, the property tax base in Columbus, Hilliard and New Albany would gain hundreds of millions in newly taxable data center value over time, which is the kind of base growth that can eventually show up as lower effective millage on everyone else's parcel, including a landlord's rental properties, once existing abatements expire or get revisited."
