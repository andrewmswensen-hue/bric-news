title: "Ohio bill would end all property tax breaks for data centers"
summary: "State Rep. Daniel Troy (D-Willowick) introduced House Bill 999, which would prohibit any state or local agency from granting property tax exemptions to data centers and would examine terminating existing abatements. Troy said the change would shift tax burden off residential property owners. A Scioto Analysis survey of 40 Ohio university economists published September 21 found 62 percent think local data center abatements are effective at attracting development, while 27 percent disagreed."
detail: "House Bill 999 would bar state and local agencies from granting new property tax exemptions to data centers and direct a review of whether existing abatements could be terminated. Troy has been working with Rep. David Thomas (R-Jefferson) on data center tax policy, though Thomas is not a cosponsor. The bill goes further than House Bill 646, an earlier proposal that would have capped data center abatements at 50 percent and required enforcement of abatement conditions; that bill stalled before the legislature's summer break over disagreements on the state's separate sales tax exemption for data centers, which Troy said has cost Ohio $1.6 billion since inception. Gov. Mike DeWine paused new grants under that sales tax exemption in early June, though existing agreements remain in place.\n\nAbatement terms vary by municipality: Columbus approved 15 years of no property tax on a Google data center in 2021, an estimated $54 million break; Hilliard's abatement saved Amazon $5.4 million in 2024; and New Albany, a hub for data center development, typically grants 10 to 15 year abatements with a minimum annual payment requirement. House Bill 999 awaits a committee assignment and is unlikely to see action before the November election."
why_it_matters: "If H.B. 999 or something like it passes, the property tax base that data center abatements currently exempt would start contributing to the same levies that fund schools and services in Franklin County, one of the few realistic paths to slowing the growth in the tax bill on everyone else's property."
